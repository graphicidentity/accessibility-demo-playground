01

IMAGES & TEXT ALTERNATIVES

The logo with no alternative

BEFORE

The brand mark is an image with no alt attribute.

→
WHY IT MATTERS

Assistive technology may announce the image filename—or skip the brand information entirely.

→
AFTER

Give an informative image concise alternative text. Use empty alt="" only when the image is decorative.

Before · missing alt

ACCESS LAB

The image above intentionally has no alt attribute.

After · useful alternative

ACCESS LAB

The image has a concise text alternative. In a real logo link, avoid repeating the same name twice.

WCAG reference: 1.1.1 Non-text Content (Level A)

02

FORMS & INPUTS

A field without a label

BEFORE

A placeholder is used instead of a persistent, programmatic label.

→
WHY IT MATTERS

The hint can disappear while typing and may not provide a reliable accessible name.

→
AFTER

Connect a visible <label> to the input with matching for and id.

Before · placeholder only

The label is visually hidden in this intentionally flawed example.

After · visible label

A visible label stays available and programmatically names the field.

WCAG reference: 3.3.2 Labels or Instructions (Level A)

03

COLOR & PERCEPTION

Text that fades into the background

BEFORE

Small text uses a muted foreground against a light surface.

→
WHY IT MATTERS

People with low vision or viewing in glare may struggle to read it.

→
AFTER

Choose colors with sufficient contrast, and verify actual ratios with a contrast checker.

Before · low contrast

Readable content should not rely on perfect eyesight or perfect lighting.

After · stronger contrast

Readable content should not rely on perfect eyesight or perfect lighting.

WCAG reference: 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) (Level AA)

04

KEYBOARD ACCESS

When focus gets stuck

BEFORE

A broken overlay keeps keyboard focus cycling within its own controls.

→
WHY IT MATTERS

Keyboard users can’t reach the rest of the page or leave the component normally.

→
AFTER

Use a well-tested dialog pattern: contain focus only while modal, support Escape, and restore focus on close.

Before · focus loop simulation

Activate the simulation, then press Tab. This safe demonstration loops between two controls; Escape always exits.

Simulation is off.

After · predictable dialog

A native dialog provides modal semantics. This example can be closed by button or Escape.

A BETTER PATTERN

Dialog with an exit

Press Escape or use the close button. Focus returns to the opener.

WCAG reference: 2.1.2 No Keyboard Trap (Level A); 2.4.3 Focus Order (Level A)

05

SCREEN READER FEEDBACK

Updates that go unheard

BEFORE

A status message appears visually, but assistive technology isn’t notified.

→
WHY IT MATTERS

People who can’t see the screen may not know whether their action worked.

→
AFTER

Use a polite live region for helpful, non-urgent updates—without moving focus.

Before · visual-only update

The message appears, but its container has no live-region semantics.

After · announced update

The status is announced politely. Screen reader output varies; test with real assistive technology.

WCAG reference: 4.1.3 Status Messages (Level AA)

06

COLOR & MEANING

Status shown by color alone

BEFORE

Green and red dots carry all the information.

→
WHY IT MATTERS

Color alone may not be distinguishable or meaningful to everyone.

→
AFTER

Add text labels or another visual cue so the message doesn’t depend on color.
